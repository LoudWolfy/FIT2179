The Threat Of Volcanoes

A Visual Exploration of Volcanic Systems, Global Risk Profiles, and the Geomorphic Threat to Australia

Chapter 1

Where They Are

Volcanoes are not distributed at random; they follow the rigid boundaries of moving tectonic plates. Below, explore how the deep geological architecture of the planet shapes our active surface features.

Mapping Prehistoric vs Active Eras

In the map above, it can be seen that the phleistocene volcanoes are not sitting on tectonic plates, this is because these are were the tectonic plates used to be. Whereas the holocene volcanoes are sitting on the tectonic plates, that is why they are considered active.

Geomorphic Typing & Boundary Engines

Subduction Zone Volcanoes are those that form on the borders of tectonic plates, Intraplate volcanoes are those that are formed within the tectonic plate Clearly the Subduction Zone volcanoes are far more common which makes sense as we know that volcanoes are usually formed on the borders of tectonic plates

Chapter 2

The Eruption Record: History & Magnitude

How do we measure the anger of a volcano? By exploring historical baselines, we can quantify the scale of historic eruptions and track the evolution of human data records over time.

The Frequency of Power

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) operates exponentially. Small eruptions occur around the globe daily, but high-magnitude 'super-eruptions' (VEI 7+) remain exceptionally rare anomalies.

The Reporting Bias Phenomenon

An initial look at history implies the planet is becoming more unstable, but the data curve actually maps the rise of communication infrastructure, satellite tracking, and global monitoring coordination. However in the heatmap we can see that most of the records are coming from the 20th century. Which is interesting and we may see a plateau in the exponential rise of volcano eruption records.

Chapter 3

Human Impact: The True Cost of Danger

The threat of a volcano is not merely a function of its physical size. True risk occurs at the intersection of geological power and human population density.

Decoupling Magnitude from Mortality

The scatter matrix above separates historical events by their explicit Volcano Explosivity Index (VEI) steps. Because death tolls vary from single digits into tens of thousands, a Logarithmic Scale is used on the vertical axis to compress values cleanly.

The Core Lethality Drivers

Most deaths come from unknown causes as can be seen by the donut chart, but when ignoring the unknown/other causes, It can be seen that most deaths are being caused by Mudflow and Pyroclastic flow. This makes sense as most people will get buried or burned by them and they are very hard to outrun

Historical Hazards in Transition

The slope metrics show how modern infrastructure and early warning systems have shifted casualty profiles. As we transition from the 19th to the 20th century, the frequency of massive catastrophe incidents drops across several main hazard domains due to improved tracking grids and planned local evacuation corridors.

Chapter 5

Australia's Safety

As can be seen here, Australia does not have many Volcanoes around it. This is due to the fact that it sits on its own tectonic plate

The Risk Next Door

Our maritime neighbors—Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and New Zealand—maintain some of the highest-density concentrations of explosive stratovolcanoes on Earth.

The Ash Infrastructure Threat

Australia’s primary threat isn't liquid lava; it is airborne ash infrastructure collapse. High-altitude injection of fine silica plume particles into regional jetstreams from a major northern eruption can instantly paralyze our national airspace and supply lines.
